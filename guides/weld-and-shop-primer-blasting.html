Weld areas and shop-primer lines: abrasive selection for shipyards

The plate body and the weld seam do not want the same abrasive. This is the media plan for the two zones that consume the most abrasive on a ship — fine grit for welds, a shot-dominant mix for the impeller line.

By Hannibal Zhang, FENGERDA Updated 19 Oct 2026 Source: detecting...

I. Why weld areas deserve their own abrasive plan

The weld area is the hardest part of a surface to treat. Weld slag, spatter and undercut create geometric irregularities that a single blast pass with standard media will not clean reliably. In secondary surface treatment at a shipyard, the weld zone is where abrasive consumption is most concentrated.

ISO 8501-3 addresses exactly this. Its P Sa 2.5 level requires the weld area to achieve a cleanliness equivalent to Sa 2.5, but reaching it takes a different media strategy from the plate around it.

II. Weld-area media and technique

Recommended abrasive: a mixture of fine-grained steel grit (HRC 55–60, G80, 0.3 mm) and standard steel grit (G40).

Technical logic: the microscopic geometry of weld undercut and pitting typically falls in the 0.2–0.5 mm range, which standard G40 grit (0.6–0.8 mm) cannot effectively penetrate. Fine G80 grit reaches these microscopic depressions for cutting and cleaning without generating excessive roughness peaks.

The working sequence. Use G40 for coarse cleaning over the large area first, then G80 for localised treatment of welds and edges. Reversing the order wastes the fine media and still leaves undercut behind.

The same two-stage logic applies to edge preparation and cut edges, which ISO 8501-3 also covers. On edges, the blast angle itself limits access, so a finer media and a closer working distance generally serve better than increasing pressure.

III. Shop-primer production lines

Shipyard steel pretreatment lines — typically roller-conveyor shot blasting machines — perform a single surface treatment on steel plate before shop primer is applied, with a target grade of Sa 2.5. The abrasive has to work in a completely different way from open-air blasting.

Recommended abrasive: steel shot (HRC 45–50, S330/S390) mixed with steel grit (HRC 55–60, G40) in a 7:3 ratio.

Technical logic: shot blasting machines use impellers to project abrasives, so the media needs higher roundness and flowability than sandblasting does. Too high a steel shot ratio gives insufficient cleaning efficiency and makes Sa 2.5 hard to reach; too high a steel grit ratio accelerates wear on the machine blades and guard plates and reduces flowability. A 7:3 ratio is the balance point most shipyards converge on, balancing abrasive life against equipment maintenance while still hitting Sa 2.5.

Roundness matters more here than on an open blast line. A production-line machine classifies and recirculates media continuously; irregular particles are rejected and lost from the working inventory far faster than in a blast cabinet. That is why a shot-dominant mix is specified for the impeller line, and why roundness is an acceptance item rather than a nicety — see the incoming inspection list on Abrasive procurement: unit price vs. total cost.

Roundness grades are quoted separately from hardness: G1 to G3 are the common commercial grades for steel shot, quoted as a suffix to the material grade rather than in place of it.

IV. Plate and weld zones on the same line

On a typical pretreatment line the two zones are not cleaned by the same media at the same moment, and combining them into one purchase specification is a common sourcing error:

ZoneTarget gradeMediaWhy
Plate bodySa 2.5 Steel shot S330/S390 (HRC 45–50) dominant, with G40 grit at about 30% High roundness for impeller projection and flow; grit supplies the cutting action needed for mill scale and light rust
Weld seams, edgesP Sa 2.5 (ISO 8501-3) Fine steel grit G80 (HRC 55–60, 0.3 mm) Reaches 0.2–0.5 mm undercut and pitting geometry that G40 cannot penetrate
Heavy scale / repair areasSa 2.5 restored Higher proportion of G40 or G25 grit Cutting action, not impact, is what removes stubborn adherent oxide

Key takeaways

  • Weld areas are not a harder version of the plate. They need finer media because the geometry to be cleaned is smaller than the media.
  • Two-stage cleaning, coarse then fine. G40 first over the area, G80 second on welds and edges.
  • Shop-primer lines are shot-dominant by design. Impeller projection demands roundness and flowability; grit above roughly 30% accelerates blade and guard plate wear.
  • Specify media per zone. A single blended media across plate and welds is cheaper to order and more expensive to run.

Frequently asked questions

Short answers drawn from the technical content above.

What abrasive is used for weld area treatment to ISO 8501-3?
A blend of fine steel grit at HRC 55-60, G80 (0.3 mm) and standard G40 grit. The undercut and pitting geometry to be cleaned is typically 0.2-0.5 mm, so 0.6-0.8 mm G40 cannot penetrate it, while G80 reaches into those depressions without creating excessive roughness peaks.
Should fine grit replace coarse grit on welds?
No, the sequence matters. Clean the surrounding area with G40 first, then apply G80 locally to the weld and edges. Doing fine first wastes the fine media on areas that coarse grit handles better, and still leaves undercut behind.
What is the right shot-to-grit ratio for a shop-primer shot blasting line?
About 7:3 steel shot to steel grit. Shot blasting machines project media with impellers, so media needs high roundness and flowability. Above that grit ratio, cleaning efficiency falls and blade and guard plate wear accelerates; below it, meeting Sa 2.5 on plate becomes difficult.
Why does the pretreatment line care about steel shot roundness?
Because the machine classifies and recirculates media continuously. Irregular particles are rejected and lost from the working inventory much faster than in a blast cabinet, so an open-air media tolerance is not good enough on a production line. Roundness is normally specified as a separate grade (G1-G3), alongside rather than instead of the material and hardness grade.
Do weld areas need their own abrasive purchase line?
Yes. Plate body targets Sa 2.5 and is shot-dominant, while weld seams and edges target P Sa 2.5 under ISO 8501-3 and need G80 fine grit. Blending them into one order simplifies purchasing but costs blast efficiency and media life.

Weld-area requirements per ISO 8501-3; plate pretreatment target Sa 2.5 per ISO 8501-1. Media recommendations reflect documented shipyard practice; machine design and coating data sheets vary, so confirm the blend against your own line data before changing a running specification.
