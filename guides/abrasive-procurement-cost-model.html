Abrasive procurement: unit price vs. total cost, and how to check quality

Two steel grit quotes, 12% apart in price and about 50% apart in reality. The gap is made of breakage rate, cycle life and first-pass yield — and every one of those can be checked on arrival.

By Hannibal Zhang, FENGERDA Updated 19 Oct 2026 Source: detecting...

I. The unit-price trap

The most common misconception in shipyard abrasive procurement is using delivered unit price as the core price comparison indicator. Two batches of steel shot that look alike can differ by 10–15% in unit price while differing far more than that in total cost.

The simplified comparison below models two steel grit offers on the same job.

Read the bottom two rows, not the top one. Supplier A is 12% cheaper per ton and roughly 50% more expensive per square metre once the shorter life, higher breakage and lower first-pass yield are counted. This is the classic abrasive procurement trap: buying cheap and using expensive.

Note how the two columns differ in kind and not only in degree. Supplier A sits in the low end of the hardness range where impact peels loose rust but does not cut, so first-pass yield drops; Supplier B cuts harder and holds its edge longer, so the same gun hours cover more surface. The rework line is what converts that into money.

II. Mandatory incoming inspection items

Acceptance of abrasive material on arrival is a crucial step in procurement quality control. The following parameters are recommended as mandatory inspection items.

Hardness sampling inspection

Randomly sample each batch and test according to GB/T 230.1 (Rockwell hardness) or GB/T 4340.1 (Vickers hardness). The pass rate should be ≥95%.

Particle size distribution

Sieve according to SAE J444 / J441. Material remaining on the main sieve should be ≥85%; sieved fines (powder) should be ≤5%. A high powder fraction is the visible symptom of the breakage behaviour that drives the cost model above.

Chemical composition

Focus on carbon, silicon, manganese, sulfur and phosphorus. Excessive sulfur and phosphorus are the main causes of brittle fracture, which is what turns a 6% breakage rate into a 12% one.

Microscopic morphology

The roundness of steel shot — the proportion of non-spherical particles should be ≤10%; and the sharpness of steel grit edges. Passivated grit significantly reduces cutting efficiency.

Cleanliness

The abrasive surface must be free of obvious oil stains (fluorescence reaction can be detected using a UV lamp), and dust content should be ≤2%. Oil carried in on the abrasive defeats the coating long before any mechanical property does.

III. Supplier evaluation dimensions

Beyond price and quality, a supplier rating card built on the following dimensions is recommended.

DimensionWhat to check
Batch stability Whether the hardness fluctuation range of three consecutive batches is within ±3 HRC. This is the single most predictive indicator of whether a delivered price will hold on site.
Supply guarantee Whether the delivery cycle is stable during peak season — March to October, the peak shipbuilding season. A supplier who is late in August is late for the whole yard.
Technical support capability Whether the supplier can provide abrasive ratio optimisation suggestions and shot blasting machine parameter debugging support, or only a price per ton.
Certifications Whether the supplier holds ISO 9001 and has classification society approved test reports.
Traceability Whether a material certificate and test report are provided for each batch, not per order.

IV. Where this is heading

  • Green shipbuilding is driving an abrasive change. With the IMO Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEXI) and Carbon Intensity Index (CII) in force, shipyards face pressure to cut emissions. The recyclability of metallic abrasives — 200 to 300 cycles — makes their carbon footprint far lower than single-use non-metallic media such as garnet or copper slag. Some EU shipyards have begun including abrasive carbon footprint in supplier evaluation.
  • Automated coating demands tighter consistency. As sandblasting robots and intelligent shot blasting equipment spread, batch consistency requirements for particle size and hardness will tighten. Automated equipment is far more sensitive to media parameters than manual operation; even small particle size fluctuations cause uneven spray coverage.
  • Ballast tank rules turn media selection into compliance. As IMO PSPC standards tighten, the first-pass yield of Sa 2.5 directly affects the 25-year target lifespan of the coating system. Abrasive selection and quality control therefore move from a cost optimisation item to a compliance risk management item.

Key takeaways

  • Never compare abrasive suppliers on price per ton. Cost per square metre and first-pass yield are the comparable numbers.
  • Hardness stability across batches is worth more than any single price concession. Ask for three-batch data before you award, not after.
  • Inspection is cheap, rework is not. Five incoming checks — hardness, grading, chemistry, morphology, cleanliness — cover most of what drives the rework line.
  • Recyclability is becoming a scored criterion. 200-300 cycles changes the carbon comparison against single-use media entirely.

How to read the numbers

The two-supplier comparison on this page is a worked model, not a measured project record: it shows the shape of the effect and the mechanism behind each line. Third-party survey figures elsewhere in this article series on rework share are industry benchmarks. Your own consumption rate, cycle life and first-pass yield should be measured on your own substrate and equipment before a contract is signed.

Frequently asked questions

Short answers drawn from the technical content above.

Why is the lowest price per ton of abrasive often the most expensive option?
Because unit price ignores breakage rate, cycle life and first-pass yield. In the worked comparison above, the lower-priced grit at HRC 50-55 lasts about 120 cycles at 12% breakage, while the higher-priced grit at HRC 58-62 lasts about 250 cycles at 6% breakage — giving 2.07 RMB/m2 against 1.35 RMB/m2 and an 88% against 96% Sa 2.5 first-pass yield, with 15-20% overall cost increase from rework.
What should be checked when abrasive arrives at the factory?
Five items: hardness by GB/T 230.1 or GB/T 4340.1 with a pass rate of at least 95%; particle size distribution to SAE J444/J441 with at least 85% on the main sieve and no more than 5% fines; chemical composition for carbon, silicon, manganese, sulfur and phosphorus; microscopic morphology including shot roundness with non-spherical particles at or below 10% and grit edge sharpness; and cleanliness with no visible oil and dust at or below 2%.
How do you judge whether an abrasive supplier is reliable?
Score them on five dimensions: batch stability, meaning the hardness fluctuation across three consecutive batches stays within about 3 HRC; supply guarantee through the March to October peak; technical support ability, meaning real mix and machine parameter advice rather than a price list; certifications such as ISO 9001 and classification society test reports; and per-batch traceability.
What hardness variation across batches should be considered acceptable?
An approximately plus or minus 3 HRC swing across three consecutive batches is a reasonable acceptance line for steel shot and grit, and it is the point at which automated blasting equipment starts producing visibly uneven coverage. It is also the same order as the tightest control offered by established European brands, so it is a fair rather than a lenient standard.
Is stainless steel or non-metallic media ever the right procurement choice?
Yes, and it is a compliance decision rather than a cost one. For stainless steel substrates, and for carbon steel being prepared ahead of a stainless steel lining, non-metallic media such as white fused alumina or garnet grit must be used to avoid embedded iron ions causing subsequent corrosion.
