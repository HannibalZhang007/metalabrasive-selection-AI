ISO 8501 & SSPC blast-cleaning grades: which abrasive each one requires

Six blast-cleaning grades, six different abrasive answers. This is the matrix that turns a paint specification into a media purchase order — with the reasoning, the particle sizes and the blend ratios behind each line.

By Hannibal Zhang, FENGERDA Updated 19 Oct 2026 Source: detecting...

I. The cost line nobody watches

Surface preparation is a crucial yet often overlooked process across the whole shipbuilding and repair lifecycle. The total steel plate area of a 300,000-tonne VLCC can exceed 200,000 m². Secondary surface treatment alone — treating welds and damaged areas after shop primer — consumes tens or even hundreds of tonnes of abrasive.

Yet in most shipyards' procurement lists, metallic abrasives are filed as auxiliary materials: low unit price, apparently simple technical parameters, easily swapped between suppliers. That framing hides the fact that abrasive selection decides whether the surface treatment passes the standard, and that it drives total shipbuilding cost through hidden paths — rework rate, paint consumption and the abrasive's own lifespan.

Where the rework figures come from. The statistics on this page — rework attributable to unsuitable abrasive selection accounting for a large share of hull-painting rework cost, and a meaningful reduction in abrasive cost per ship after switching to a suitable product — are drawn from a third-party shipbuilding research survey, not from Fengerda project records. Treat them as industry-level benchmarks. The grade-to-abrasive recommendations and process parameters below reflect standard requirements and documented shop practice.

II. ISO 8501: visual assessment grades

ISO 8501-1 is the internationally recognised and most widely used visual assessment standard for rust and pretreatment grades of steel surfaces, originally derived from the Swedish standard SIS 05 5900. Its structure is:

2.1 Original rust grade of the substrate

Every Sa grade is judged against the condition the steel arrived in. A surface assessed to Sa 2½ will not look the same on Grade A steel and on Grade D steel.

Original rust gradeDefinitionTypical scenario
Grade A Brand new steel surface covered with a complete oxide scale, rust-free Steel mill condition
Grade B Rusting has begun, oxide scale partially peeling off Stored outdoors for several weeks
Grade C Oxide scale completely peeled off due to rust, widespread pitting corrosion visible Long-term exposure
Grade D Severe pitting corrosion, visible porous rust pits Long-term exposure to marine environments

2.2 Pretreatment grades after blasting

GradeDefinition and typical use
Sa1
Light blast cleaning		 Removes only loose scale, rust and coating; a significant amount of residue remains on the surface. Suitable for general corrosion protection requirements.
Sa2
Thorough blast cleaning		 Removes almost all mill scale, rust and coatings; may leave spot or streak-like shadows on the surface. Suitable for pretreatment of conventional marine coating systems.
Sa2.5
Very thorough blast cleaning		 No visible oil, mill scale, rust or coating residue; only trace amounts of spot or streak-like colour differences are permitted. This is the most commonly used grade for marine coatings — almost all mainstream systems (epoxy zinc-rich, polyurethane, epoxy micaceous iron oxide) require Sa 2.5 as the minimum pretreatment.
Sa3
Blast cleaning to visually clean steel		 Surface has a uniform metallic sheen with no visible residue. Extremely expensive; used only in special applications such as stainless steel linings and thermal spray pretreatment.
Weld areas are governed separately. ISO 8501-3 is a supplementary standard to ISO 8501-1, written specifically for the surface treatment assessment of welds, cut edges and other defective areas. It recognises that oxide scale, weld slag and undercut in the weld zone are fundamentally different from defects in the flat plate zone, and defines three separate treatment levels — P Sa1 / P Sa2 / P Sa2.5 — for the weld area. In secondary surface treatment the weld region has the highest abrasive consumption of any area on the ship.

III. SSPC: the North American equivalent

The SSPC standard system (now SSPC: The Society for Protective Coatings, merged with NACE to form AMPP) is widely used in North America and among shipowners applying US standards. Its blast-cleaning standards correspond to ISO grades as follows:

SSPC standardNameApproximate ISO levelTypical application
SSPC-SP7Brush-off Blast CleaningSa1Minor treatment of existing intact coatings
SSPC-SP6Commercial Blast CleaningSa2Conventional industrial corrosion protection
SSPC-SP10Near-White Blast CleaningSa2.5Marine ballast tanks, cargo oil tanks, seawater tanks
SSPC-SP5White Metal Blast CleaningSa3Thermal spraying, special linings

SSPC-SP10 (Near-White) is the most critical grade in marine coating. It requires that at least95% of the surface area per square inch be free of visible residue, with the remaining 5% allowing only trace amounts of colour difference or shading. This standard is directly referenced by the IMO PSPC (Performance Standard for Protective Coatings) and has become a mandatory requirement for pretreatment before coating all seawater ballast tanks.

IV. Cleanliness is only half of the specification

Surface treatment is not only about being clean, but about achieving the specified surface roughness (profile / anchor pattern), which directly affects coating adhesion.

  • ISO 8503 — surface roughness of steel surfaces after shot blasting is assessed using comparative samples, categorised into Fine, Medium and Coarse grades.
  • ASTM D4417 — peak-to-valley height is measured with a surface profilometer (expressed as Hpy or Rz).

Typical roughness requirements for marine epoxy coatings are medium roughness, Rz 50–85 µm. Excessive coarseness increases paint consumption and risks failure to cover peaks; excessive fineness leaves insufficient anchor pattern engagement.

V. Grade-to-abrasive correspondence

Based on ISO 8501 and SSPC requirements, mainstream marine coating technical data sheets and documented shop practice, the selection matrix below is the part most often missing from a procurement specification.

Sa2 / SSPC-SP6 — thorough blast cleaning

This grade allows slight colour differences and suits general industrial corrosion protection and medium-grade systems for hull plates.

Recommended abrasive: medium-hardness steel shot (HRC 40–50), grit size S330 or S390 (1.0–1.2 mm).

Technical logic: the impact of spherical steel shot effectively removes oxide scale and loose rust while forming a uniform indentation-type anchor pattern. Medium-hardness shot does not break easily after impact and can be recycled 200–300 times, with one tonne treating 30–40 m². For stubborn oxide scale, add 30–40% steel grit (HRC 55–60, G40) to improve cutting efficiency.

Cost characteristic: long abrasive life and low breakage rate (3–5% replenishment per cycle) make this the most cost-effective option.

Sa2.5 / SSPC-SP10 — very thorough blast cleaning

This is the core grade for marine coatings, requiring near-complete cleanliness. Suitable for pretreatment in harsh corrosive environments such as ballast tanks, cargo oil tanks, freshwater tanks and seawater cooling pipe systems.

Recommended abrasive: primarily steel grit (HRC 55–65, G40/G25, 0.6–1.0 mm), mixed with 20–30% steel shot (HRC 50–55, S330).

Technical logic: the angular cutting action of steel grit is what achieves Sa 2.5 cleanliness — spherical shot relies on impact peeling, which is insufficient for stubborn deposits in weld undercuts and pitting pits. Grit penetrates deep into microscopic uneven areas and removes rust products at the bottom of pits. The shot is added to maintain anchor pattern uniformity, because pure grit produces overly sharp peaks and valleys with insufficient coating coverage.

Particle size must match the roughness requirement: Rz 50–85 µm is stably achieved with G40 grit (0.6–0.8 mm) at 0.6–0.7 MPa blasting pressure. For higher requirements such as thick-film coatings at Rz 75–100 µm, choose G25 (0.8–1.0 mm).

Cost characteristic: grit has a shorter life than shot (higher hardness means higher breakage, requiring 5–8% replenishment per cycle) but offers high cleaning efficiency and low rework rate. Abrasive cost typically accounts for no more than 15% of total surface treatment cost; rework cost is the real drain. Choosing the right abrasive saves far more on rework than the price difference it costs.

Sa3 / SSPC-SP5 — blast cleaning to visually clean steel

This grade requires a uniform metallic lustre with no visible residue. It is used only in special applications such as thermal spraying and stainless steel linings, and rarely on a large scale in shipyards.

Recommended abrasive: high-hardness steel grit (HRC 60–66, G50/G40), or white fused alumina (46#–60#).

Technical logic: Sa 3 demands extremely high cutting efficiency, and high-hardness grit removes all adhering substances quickly. However, for stainless steel substrates, or scenarios sensitive to iron-ion contamination such as treating carbon steel before a stainless steel lining, non-metallic abrasives (white fused alumina or garnet grit) must be used to avoid iron ions embedding in the substrate and causing subsequent corrosion.

Cost characteristic: consumption is high (grit breakage can exceed 10%) and labour cost is high, so it is applied locally only.

How to read the hardness figures above. They are the descriptive working ranges for each process step, not product grade codes. Fengerda's commercial steel shot and grit grading follows the standard letter codes: GP 42–52 HRC, GL 52–61 HRC, GH >61 HRC. When a specification states a range such as HRC 55–60, map it to the code band that contains it rather than treating the range as a grade name.

Key takeaways

  • Sa 2.5 / SSPC-SP10 is the working grade of marine coating. Specifying a lower grade and hoping is the most common cause of coating failure.
  • Cleanliness and roughness are separate specifications. Quote ISO 8503 or ASTM D4417 alongside the Sa grade, not instead of it.
  • Grade determines abrasive type, not just abrasive price. Sa 2 wants shot; Sa 2.5 wants grit with shot blended in; Sa 3 needs high-hardness grit or non-metallic media.
  • Weld areas have their own standard. ISO 8501-3 governs them, and they consume more abrasive than the plate around them.

How the recommendations are built

Three levels of evidence appear on this page and should not be read as equivalent: (1) standard definitions quoted from ISO 8501-1, ISO 8501-3, ISO 8503, ASTM D4417 and the SSPC catalogue; (2) industry survey figures on rework share and cost reduction, which are third-party and directional; (3) process recommendations derived from standard requirements, coating data sheets and documented shop practice — these are engineering guidance, not a substitute for trial on your own substrate.

Frequently asked questions

Short answers drawn from the technical content above.

What abrasives are needed for ISO 8501-3 and SSPC-SP10?
For Sa 2.5 / SSPC-SP10 ballast and cargo tank work, the standard specification is a predominantly angular steel grit at HRC 55-65, G40 or G25 (0.6-1.0 mm), blended with 20-30% steel shot at HRC 50-55 (S330) to keep the anchor pattern uniform. For Sa 2 / SSPC-SP6 use medium-hardness steel shot at HRC 40-50, S330 or S390, optionally with 30-40% G40 grit added for stubborn scale. Weld areas under ISO 8501-3 need finer grit than the surrounding plate, typically G80 at HRC 55-60.
What is the difference between ISO 8501-1 and ISO 8501-3?
ISO 8501-1 covers blast-cleaning and pretreatment grades for the flat plate area. ISO 8501-3 is a supplementary standard for welds, cut edges and other defective areas, because weld slag, undercut and spatter are fundamentally different from plate defects. It defines its own levels: P Sa1, P Sa2 and P Sa2.5. A specification that quotes only ISO 8501-1 has not specified weld treatment.
Is SSPC-SP10 the same as Sa2.5?
Yes in practice. SSPC-SP10 (Near-White) corresponds to ISO Sa 2.5 and requires at least 95% of the surface per square inch to be free of visible residue, with only trace colour difference in the remaining 5%. SSPC-SP6 corresponds to Sa 2, SP7 to Sa 1, and SP5 to Sa 3.
Do I need abrasive hardness above 60 HRC for Sa2.5?
No, and high hardness is not free. A predominantly high-hardness medium such as HRC 60-66 raises the breakage rate and shortens life. Sa 2.5 is normally reached with G40 or G25 grit at HRC 55-65. Hardness above 60 HRC belongs to Sa 3 work, where cutting efficiency matters more than cost, or to special applications.
Which abrasive should be used for stainless steel or before a stainless lining?
Non-metallic abrasive. Steel grit or shot leaves embedded iron ions in the substrate, which can initiate corrosion under the stainless lining. Use white fused alumina (46#-60#) or garnet grit for stainless steel substrates and for carbon steel being prepared ahead of a stainless lining.
How much abrasive does one tonne of steel shot cover?
For Sa 2 work with medium-hardness S330 or S390 shot, one tonne treats roughly 30-40 square metres. Coverage falls sharply as hardness and breakage rise, and for Sa 2.5 with grit the consumption rate is materially higher. This is the number that actually drives cost, and it is covered in Abrasive procurement: unit price vs. total cost.
