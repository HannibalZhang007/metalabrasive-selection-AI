How to choose the right abrasive media for your application

A step-by-step decision path from material, profile and equipment to a concrete media shortlist.

Read full article

Choosing the right abrasive media comes down to six questions, answered in order:

1. What is your base material?

  • Ferrous (carbon steel, cast iron) — the widest media family is available: steel shot (A/B), steel grit (C/D), grinding shot (F/G/H/N) and recycled cut wire (E).
  • Stainless steel — you must use stainless shot (J/K/L/M) or stainless cut wire (O/P) to avoid carbon contamination that causes rust spots.
  • Non-ferrous (aluminum, copper, zinc) — copper cut wire (I) or stainless media; never carbon steel media (causes iron smut and galvanic corrosion).

2. What surface treatment do you need?

  • Cleaning / rust removal — spherical media (steel shot A, or stainless shot J for stainless bases).
  • Descaling / roughening — angular media (steel grit C, or bearing grit D for longer life).
  • Shot peening — spherical media only, to induce residual compressive stress; steel shot A for general parts, grinding shot G82B (H-grade) for heavy-duty peening.
  • Precision cleaning / polishing — fine spherical media, low power.

3. Target roughness Rz?

Rz determines particle size. For steel shot: S170 (<50 μm), S230 (50-80), S280 (50-100), S330/S390 (80-120+). For steel grit: G80 → G16 as roughness increases. Stainless and cut wire use diameter φ (0.4 mm → 1.4 mm).

4. Equipment type?

Wheel blasting (抛丸机) handles both shot and grit. Pressure blasting (喷砂机) with shot peening must use grit only — never shot or grinding shot (they jam and break the nozzle).

5. Hardness grade?

For steel shot/grit: GP (42-52 HRC, general), GL (52-61 HRC, shot peening), GH (>61 HRC, special high-wear). Stainless, copper, cut wire and grinding shot do not use this scale.

6. Annual throughput?

Multiply throughput (tons) × industry consumption rate (kg/ton) ÷ 1000 × recovery factor. With a recovery system the factor is 1; without it, 1.6 (you consume 60% more). ≥300 t/yr qualifies for on-site visit and custom blending.

Blast media price vs. total blasting cost

Why cheap media is rarely the cheapest option — and how to calculate the difference honestly.

Read full article

Most buyers compare media on price per kg. That is the single biggest mistake in abrasive procurement. Here is why:

The real formula

Cost per ton of workpiece = (media unit price × tons consumed + power + labor + equipment depreciation & maintenance + dust control − scrap recovery) ÷ workpiece tons

Why cheaper media costs more

  • Higher consumption — low-quality steel shot shatters after 500-800 cycles vs. 1500-2000 for GP-grade. You buy 2-3× more kg to blast the same ton of steel.
  • More downtime — broken media clogs the recovery system, wears blades and causes more frequent media changes.
  • Higher dust cost — shattered fines increase dust load, raising filter replacement and disposal cost.
  • Lower scrap value — good steel shot retains 40-60% of its value as recyclable scrap; broken fines are near-worthless.

Worked example

Steel structure plant, 5,000 t/yr throughput, wheel blasting, with recovery:

  • Scenario A (cheap shot, $0.60/kg): 0.6 kg/t × 5000 t = 3 t consumed → media cost $1,800; higher power + dust + downtime → total cost per ton ≈ $2.10.
  • Scenario B (GP-grade shot, $0.90/kg): 0.4 kg/t × 5000 t = 2 t consumed → media cost $1,800; lower power + dust + downtime → total cost per ton ≈ $1.55.
  • Result: Scenario B saves $0.55/ton = $2,750/yr, even though the media costs 50% more per kg.

Sa 2½ vs. Sa 3: when does "cleaner" stop paying off?

Matching surface cleanliness to the coating system — and where over-specification quietly burns budget.

Read full article

ISO 8501-1 defines four blast-cleaning grades. Specifying a higher grade than your coating requires is one of the most common — and most expensive — mistakes in surface preparation.

When each grade is right

  • Sa 1 (light): Maintenance blasting, removing loose paint and rust before recoating. Rarely specified for new work.
  • Sa 2 (thorough): General maintenance, non-critical structural steel, shop primer application.
  • Sa 2½ (very thorough): The industry default for most coating systems — epoxy primers, zinc-rich primers, polyurethane topcoats. 90% of new structural steel spec is Sa 2½.
  • Sa 3 (visually clean steel): Required only for thermal spraying (metallizing), high-performance tank linings, and some nuclear/aerospace applications. Demands clean, dry abrasive and a controlled environment.

Why over-specifying burns budget

  • Media consumption doubles going from Sa 2½ to Sa 3 — you blast the same surface twice to remove the last "shadows."
  • Roughness climbs — Sa 3 on a profile-sensitive coating (e.g. thin-film epoxy) can push Rz above the coating's tolerance, causing pinholes.
  • Reblast risk — if the surface flash-rusts before coating (especially outdoors), you reblast to Sa 3 again, doubling cost.

The decision rule

Always spec the lowest grade your coating supplier will accept. If the data sheet says "Sa 2½, Rz 50-80 μm," do not upgrade to Sa 3 "to be safe" — it adds 40-60% to your blasting cost with zero coating benefit. Reserve Sa 3 for thermal spray and critical tank linings where the spec demands it.

Bottom line: Check the coating data sheet first, spec the grade it requires, and confirm with your abrasive supplier that your media + equipment can hit that grade + profile consistently.

Recyclable vs. single-use media: the real consumption math

Comparing steel shot, steel grit and expendable media across real recycling rates.

Read full article

The two media families are bought on completely different models, and comparing them on price per kg is the fastest route to a wrong answer.

1. Two different purchase models

  • Recyclable metallic media — steel shot (A/B), steel grit (C/D), cut wire (E), grinding shot (F/G/H) and stainless media (J/K/L/M, O/P). You buy a working inventory once, then top it up. With a separator in place, consumption runs at roughly 5% per cycle, so recovery rates above 95% are normal.
  • Single-use expendable media — garnet, aluminium oxide, copper slag and similar. Nothing is recovered: whatever you blast is what you buy, every time.

So the real question is never “what does it cost per kg?” but “how many kg do I have to buy to process one ton of workpiece?”

2. The formula that makes them comparable

Annual consumption (t) = throughput (t/yr) × industry rate (kg/ton) ÷ 1000 × recovery factor

The recovery factor is where most comparisons go wrong. With a working magnetic and air separation system, use 1.0. Without one, use 1.6 — you consume about 60% more media, because broken fines and perfectly usable media both leave the machine together.

This is also the cheapest upgrade available on most lines. Adding magnetic plus air separation typically cuts consumption by 30% or more, and pays for itself within the first year on any operation above a few hundred tons annually.

3. What “recyclable” actually depends on

  • Hardness class and consistency — for steel shot and grit the scale is GP (42-52 HRC, general purpose), GL (52-61 HRC, shot peening) and GH (>61 HRC, special high-wear). Select by duty, not by “harder must be better”.
  • Batch-to-batch tolerance — media whose hardness swings widely between deliveries breaks unpredictably. A band of ±3-5 HRC is the practical norm across established producers. Treat anything wider as a risk, and always ask for the batch certificate rather than a single nominal figure.
  • Internal soundness — hollow, porous or cracked particles shatter early and are lost as dust. High-density integrity above 95% is the benchmark worth writing into a specification.
  • The machine side — separator setting, dust extraction and blade wear decide whether good media actually gets recycled. Equipment older than roughly eight years usually needs one particle size grade dropped and an impeller wear check.

4. Where single-use media still makes sense

Being straight about this makes the rest of the argument land harder. Expendable media remains the right call when:

  • Recovery is physically impossible — remote field work, one-off outdoor blasting, awkward geometry.
  • The campaign is too short to justify charging the machine with a working inventory.
  • Metallic media is ruled out on contamination grounds — for stainless bases use stainless media (J/K/L/M) or stainless cut wire (O/P); for non-ferrous use copper cut wire (I). Carbon steel media causes iron smut and galvanic corrosion and should never be used there.

Outside those cases, single-use media is usually a cost decision that was never actually calculated.

5. Worked example

Shipyard panel line, 5,000 t/yr throughput, wheel blasting, separator installed.

  • Scenario A — recyclable steel shot, GP (42-52 HRC), $0.90/kg
    Industry rate 0.5 kg/ton × 5,000 t = 2.5 t/yr → media cost $2,250, plus scrap value recovered on the fines.
  • Scenario B — single-use expendable, $0.35/kg
    Nothing recovered, consumption 8 kg/ton × 5,000 t = 40 t/yr → media cost $14,000, before disposal and dust control.

The expendable media is 61% cheaper per kg and roughly 6× more expensive per year. Now take the separator away from Scenario A: the recovery factor rises to 1.6, consumption goes to 4.0 t/yr and the cost to $3,600 — still close to four times cheaper than the one-pass option, and repairing the separator brings it straight back to $2,250.

Assumptions above are illustrative. Substitute your own unit prices, industry rate and throughput — the ranking is what matters, not the absolute figures.

6. How to verify a recyclability claim

Ask any supplier for these four items. One who cannot produce them is guessing.

  • Sieve analysis before and after a known number of cycles — particle size distribution is the direct evidence of breakdown.
  • A hardness certificate stating the batch range in HRC rather than one nominal value.
  • A density or microstructure statement — this is what separates sound media from media that shatters.
  • A trial protocol — run a defined tonnage, weigh the makeup media added, and divide. That gives your true consumption rate in kg/ton, measured on your own machine.
